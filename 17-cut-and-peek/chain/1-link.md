## KNOWN

The five-card trick (den Boer, 1989) computes AND with five cards and one random cut; a four-card non-committed AND using a random bisection cut is due to Mizuki, Kumamaru and Sone (2012). Lower bounds on card counts under restrictions on shuffles (uniform, closed, finite runtime) were given by Koch, Walzer and Härtel and by Kastner et al.; shuffle counts have been attacked by bounded model checking (Koch, Schrempp, Kirsten). Single-shuffle protocols that open every card have been studied (a six-card three-input equality with one random cut; a link to private simultaneous messages by Shinagawa and Nuida, I believe). The seed is not flawed but is a family of questions, not one: "a given set of shuffles", "a given function", committed or non-committed output, finite or Las Vegas runtime are all left open, so it has no unique answer as stated.

## SUCCESSOR QUESTION

Fix n ≥ 2. A one-shuffle open protocol with m cards consists of public data: a partition of the positions {1,…,m} into sets P_1,…,P_n,H (each P_i non-empty, H possibly empty); for each player i two words u_i^0, u_i^1 in {♣,♥}^{P_i}; a helper word h in {♣,♥}^H; and a permutation π of {1,…,m}. Cards with the same face are indistinguishable, all backs are identical, and any number of cards of each face is available.

Play. Each player i holds a secret bit x_i and, unseen by the others, lays the word u_i^{x_i} face down on the positions P_i; the word h is laid face down on H. This gives a row w(x) in {♣,♥}^m. Then exactly one shuffle happens: an integer j is drawn uniformly from {0,…,ord(π)−1}, nobody learns j, and the row is rearranged by π^j (the card at position p moves to position π^j(p)). Then all m cards are turned face up and everyone sees the resulting row s. Nothing else happens.

The protocol securely computes AND if (correctness) there is a function g with g(s) = x_1 ∧ … ∧ x_n for every input x and every j, and (privacy) for any two inputs x, x′ with the same AND, the probability distributions of s are identical.

Example: π the 5-cycle p → p+1 mod 5 is a random cut; with P_1={1,2}, H={3}, P_2={4,5}, u_1^0=♥♣, u_1^1=♣♥, h=♥, u_2^0=♣♥, u_2^1=♥♣ one gets the classical five-card trick, a valid protocol for n=2.

Let c(n) be the least m for which a one-shuffle open protocol securely computing the n-bit AND exists, and c(n)=∞ if none exists for any m. Determine c(n): give the exact values of c(2), c(3) and c(4), and for general n either a formula for c(n) or a proof that c(n)=∞ from some n onward.

## WHY IT IS PROBABLY UNCHARTED

Known minimality results concern two-input AND with multi-step protocols, or single-shuffle protocols with arbitrary (non-cyclic) shuffles, where generic constructions exist. Restricting to one shuffle drawn from a cyclic group generated by any permutation sits between "random cut only" and "any shuffle", and I know no result giving the card count for n-bit AND there. Doubts: single-cut full-open protocols have a small literature I recall imperfectly; an impossibility for n ≥ 3 under random cuts may be known, and it might extend easily to cyclic groups, making the answer "5, ∞, ∞".

## HOW TO CHECK AN ANSWER

The opened row is uniform on the orbit of w(x) under the group generated by π, so a protocol is valid exactly when all inputs with AND 0 give rows in one orbit and the all-ones input gives a row in a different orbit. Only the cycle type of π matters (relabel positions). For each m, enumerate cycle types, partitions of positions and words, and test the orbit condition; this confirms an upper bound by exhibiting a protocol and a lower bound by exhaustion for small m. A claim of ∞ needs a proof; computation can only refute it.
