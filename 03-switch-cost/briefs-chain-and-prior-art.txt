THE QUESTION YOU RECEIVE

A single processor serves jobs that arrive online. Time is discrete. Each job arrives at some tick with an integer length (ticks of work it needs) and an integer deadline. An adversary chooses the arrivals, seeing the schedule so far, but may only issue job sets that a clairvoyant scheduler could finish on time if switching between jobs were free. The online scheduler may preempt a job and resume it later, but every time it switches from one unfinished job to a different job the processor is idle for c ticks first (a context switch). Starting a job after another has finished is free. The online scheduler is given a processor that is s times faster than the clairvoyant one. For each switch cost c: what is the smallest speed s(c) with which some online scheduler meets every deadline against every adversary? How does it grow with c, and what does the best scheduler look like (when is it right to refuse to preempt)?

Fix integers P >= 1 (maximum job length) and c >= 1 (switch cost). Time is ticks 0, 1, 2, ....

- Scheduler. In each tick the processor runs one unit of one job, or idles, or spends a switch tick. Let A be the job most recently run. Running a job B != A while A is unfinished requires that the c ticks immediately before B's unit were switch ticks. If A is finished, or nothing has run yet, starting or resuming any job is free. Idling does not change A.
- Adversary. It is adaptive and sees the schedule so far. It may release any finite number of jobs per tick, subject to one rule: at all times, for all integers a < b, the total length of released jobs with a <= r and d <= b is at most b - a. That is, a scheduler for whom switching is free could meet every deadline.
- Value. A job's lateness is max(0, completion time - d), where completion time is the tick index after its last unit. L(c, P) is the least integer L such that some online scheduler guarantees lateness <= L for every job against every adversary.

Questions. Determine the sequence a(P) = L(1, P) for P = 1, 2, 3, ..., and then L(c, P) in general.
- Is a(P) = P - 1 always, meaning never preempting is optimal even when a switch costs a single tick?
- Or is a(P) = o(P), for example of order sqrt(P), from balancing refused preemptions against accumulated switch ticks?
- For which (c, P) does L(c, P) first equal its never-preempt value?
- What rule tells the optimal scheduler to refuse a preemption?

(Background from the previous thinker: the original question asked how much faster a processor must be to meet every deadline when each preemption costs c idle ticks; that is infinite for c >= 1, because a one-tick job with a one-tick window can arrive mid-job. Can the adversary repeat or nest the trick so that lateness accumulates without bound (making L infinite), or does the load rule stop that? (ii) Is "never preempt" really bounded by P - 1, and is it really forced? (iii) For fixed (c, P), is there an honest finite game: can windows, the number of pending jobs and the backlog be bounded without changing the value? If the game as given is trivial, infinite or not finitely checkable, repair it in your successor with ONE change, and say what you changed and why.

==== Prior-art search switch-cost lateness game ====
Prior-art search. I need to know whether some specific small results are already in the published literature, or are close corollaries of something that is. Use web search. Be skeptical in both directions: do not declare something novel because a quick search found nothing, and do not declare it known because a paper sounds similar. Never invent a citation: every paper you name must come from a search result you actually saw, with its URL.

THE SETTING

Online single-machine scheduling in discrete time, as a worst-case game. Jobs (release r, length p <= P, deadline d) are released by an adaptive adversary. The online scheduler may preempt, but leaving an unfinished job for another costs one idle tick (a context switch / preemption overhead); starting a job after another finishes is free. The objective is the maximum lateness max(0, completion - d) over all jobs. The adversary is bound by a promise: the set of jobs released so far must always be feasible (all deadlines met) for a clairvoyant schedule. Two versions: the clairvoyant schedule switches for FREE (ordinary preemptive feasibility), value F(P); or the clairvoyant schedule pays the SAME one-tick switch cost, value S(P).

CLAIMS
 1. F(P) <= P - 1 for every P: non-preemptive earliest-deadline-first is at most P - 1 late on any preemptively feasible job set with job lengths <= P.
 2. Exact small values by exhaustive game solving: S(2) = F(2) = 1, S(3) = F(3) = 2, S(4) = F(4) = 3. So for P <= 4 the right to preempt at a cost of one tick is worth NOTHING to an online scheduler in the worst case: never preempting is optimal, even when the benchmark pays the same cost.
 3. Conjecture: S(P) = F(P) = P - 1 for all P.
 4. The adversary needs a longer horizon under the same-cost promise (4, 9, about 15 ticks for P = 2, 3, 4) than under the free promise (3, 6, 10).

WHAT TO LOOK FOR
 1. Claim 1 is surely classical: find the standard reference for the blocking bound of non-preemptive EDF (maximum lateness / tardiness bounded by the longest job; Jeffay, Stanat, Martel 1991 "On non-preemptive scheduling of periodic and sporadic tasks"; George, Rivierre, Spuri; "non-preemptive EDF tardiness bound").
 2. Online scheduling with preemption penalties / preemption overhead / context-switch cost / setup cost on preemption: competitive analysis ("online scheduling with preemption penalties", Zheng, Xu, Zhang; Fung; "preemption with restart"; "scheduling with bounded preemption cost"), limited-preemption real-time scheduling (Buttazzo, Bertogna, Yao: "limited preemptive scheduling"; "preemption threshold"; "floating non-preemptive regions"; Baruah "the limited-preemption uniprocessor scheduling of sporadic task systems"), and any LOWER BOUND showing an online scheduler cannot benefit from costly preemption, or an optimality result for non-preemptive EDF among online schedulers with preemption overhead.
 3. Online minimisation of maximum lateness or tardiness against an adversary (rather than throughput or flow time), with or without resource augmentation; "online deadline scheduling maximum lateness lower bound"; "non-clairvoyant" is a different thing (unknown lengths) but check.
 4. Any work comparing an online algorithm against an offline optimum that pays the same switching/preemption cost versus one that does not.
 5. Related models where changing what you work on costs time: online scheduling with setup times / changeover, "job switching cost", polling systems worst case.
